Fraud Allegations and § 523(a) Carve-Outs

Most EIDL discharges go through routinely. The exceptions are fraud-flagged loans — loans where the SBA OIG (Office of Inspector General) or DOJ alleges that the loan was obtained or used by fraud. These trigger § 523(a)(2)(A), (a)(2)(B), (a)(4), or (a)(6) and may produce a nondischargeable judgment.

The four exceptions

§ 523(a)(2)(A) — false pretenses, false representation, actual fraud

Common fact patterns:

  • Misrepresenting revenue on Form 5 (sole proprietor) or Form 4506-T (tax-return verification)
  • Reporting employees who didn't exist
  • Inflating cost-of-goods-sold to qualify for higher loan amount
  • Listing a business that wasn't actually operating in February 2020 (the COVID-EIDL pre-pandemic operations test)

Standard: the SBA must prove (1) the misrepresentation was material, (2) you knew it was false (or were reckless about its truth), (3) you intended for SBA to rely on it, (4) SBA's reliance was justifiable, and (5) SBA suffered damages.

§ 523(a)(2)(B) — materially false written statement of financial condition

Same general framework but specifically about written financial statements. SBA Form 2202 (Schedule of Liabilities), Form 1919 (Borrower Information), and Form 4506-T (tax-return request) are the typical exhibits.

Higher bar than (a)(2)(A): the statement must be in writing, must concern the debtor's financial condition (or that of an insider), and the creditor must have reasonably relied.

§ 523(a)(4) — fraud or defalcation in fiduciary capacity

Rarely applies to EIDL because the borrower is not a fiduciary to SBA. Sometimes invoked where loan covenants restrict the use of funds and the borrower violated a use restriction with intent. Generally a thin theory for EIDL.

§ 523(a)(6) — willful and malicious injury

Comes up where the borrower took the loan proceeds, intentionally diverted them to non-business uses (e.g., personal investment, real estate unrelated to the business), and the diversion is characterized as a willful and malicious injury to SBA's interest in the funds.

Standard: Kawaauhau v. Geiger, 523 U.S. 57 (1998) — requires both willfulness AND malice; negligence or reckless conduct is not enough.

The OIG investigation timeline

From OIG's public statistics: more than 12,000 EIDL fraud investigations opened since 2021, with a multi-year backlog. If you received an OIG subpoena, CID, or interview request, you are in the investigation queue. Bankruptcy does not stop a criminal investigation (§ 362(b)(1)) but does stop civil collection.

If you have an OIG investigation, get counsel

This page is educational only. Cases involving SBA OIG, civil investigative demands, or DOJ civil/criminal contact need a bankruptcy attorney coordinating with white-collar criminal defense counsel. The bankruptcy strategy depends on whether discharge will be challenged and on the underlying criminal exposure.
