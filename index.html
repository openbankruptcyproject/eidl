EIDL Loan Discharge in Bankruptcy

Plain-language educational guide on Economic Injury Disaster Loan (EIDL) discharge under federal bankruptcy law. Covers personal-guarantee thresholds, fraud carve-outs under § 523(a)(2)/(4)/(6), Treasury Offset Program exposure, and chapter-by-chapter outcomes. Educational content only; not legal advice.

The single most important question

Did you sign a personal guarantee on the EIDL? EIDL loans under $200,000 generally did not require a personal guarantee. Loans of $200,000 or more did. If your loan was below the threshold and you signed no personal guarantee, the loan is the obligation of the business entity only — you have no individual liability and individual bankruptcy generally has no effect on the loan.

The basic discharge framework

An EIDL is an unsecured (or partially secured) general business debt issued by the U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA). For bankruptcy discharge purposes it is treated like any other federal-government debt: generally dischargeable in Chapter 7 unless one of the § 523(a) exceptions applies.

The exceptions that come up with EIDL:

  • § 523(a)(2)(A) — obtaining the loan by false pretenses or actual fraud (e.g., misrepresenting revenue, employee count, or business existence)
  • § 523(a)(2)(B) — materially false written statement of financial condition (e.g., the SBA Form 1919, IRS 4506-T)
  • § 523(a)(4) — fraud or defalcation while acting in a fiduciary capacity (rare for EIDL)
  • § 523(a)(6) — willful and malicious injury (e.g., misuse of loan proceeds with intent to harm SBA)

The SBA Office of Inspector General (OIG) has investigated thousands of EIDL fraud cases since 2021. If your loan is part of an OIG investigation, fraud carve-outs may apply.
